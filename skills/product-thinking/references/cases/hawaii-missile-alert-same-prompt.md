**Decision.** On January 13, 2018, a supervisor ran an unannounced missile drill at a shift change. The recorded drill message said "exercise" but also said "this is not a drill". The warning officer believed the attack was real. He picked the live alert template from a drop-down menu and clicked yes on a prompt asking if he was sure. The software showed the same prompts for test and live alerts. Its prepared text let staff click through without reading the warning that would go out. One person could send an alert alone, and there was no template to retract a false alert.

**Outcome.** The alert reached phones across Hawaii at 8:07 a.m. The correction went out at 8:45 a.m., 38 minutes later, because no standard way to send it existed. The FCC said the state had no reasonable safeguards. Hawaii then required two officers to approve every alert and test, added a false alarm template, and required advance notice of drills.

**Lesson.** If a test action and a live action look the same and cannot be undone, the interface must make the difference unmistakable and provide a fast way to recover.
