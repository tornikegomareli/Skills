**Claim.** A product that competes on its own features, instead of on what users become able to do, gives users little to show and little reason to stay or recommend it.

## Why it is a problem
Kathy Sierra says to compete on user awesome, not app awesome. When users praise a product, they mean it helped them get better at something they care about. She calls that bigger goal the compelling context: the photographer wants great photos, not a better camera menu. She also names the "suck zone", the early period where new users struggle and most quit. Products should show that this stage is normal and give real wins earlier. Jobs made the same turn in 1997: Apple's strategy began with what benefit it could give the customer and where it could take them.

## How to detect
- Read the README, landing page, and changelog. Count lines about features against lines about what the user can now do. Flag a feature-only balance.
- Check whether the product names the bigger goal its users have (ship a site, close a deal, learn a skill). No named goal is a flag.
- Walk onboarding. Flag it if the first session ends without a real result the user can keep or show.
- Look for examples, templates, or galleries of good results. Sierra says users learn by seeing many good examples. None is a flag.
- Check analytics for an event that marks a user result, not only clicks or sessions.
- Intake question: "What can your best users do now that they could not do before?"

## Fixes
- Write the user's bigger goal in one sentence. Rewrite the README intro and changelog around results toward it.
- Move the first real win into the first session. Cut steps that come before it.
- Add a set of good examples or templates. Show what an expert result looks like.

## Cases
- `google-glass-explorer-vs-enterprise` (failure)
- `canva-design-for-non-designers` (success)
